# Long Friday

Mum didn't work Fridays. That's what we always told people, anyway.

**6:52 a.m.**

The first thing I noticed was the quiet: the absence of noises I hadn't known were there. The kettle. The toaster's spring. A knife scraping Vegemite across bread. The radio telling nobody about traffic on the Monash.

Mum was on the back step with a cup of coffee. One cup.

"There's no bread out," Dad said, holding the fridge open as if the bread might be hiding behind the milk.

"I'm having a day off," Mum said, without turning around.

"A day off from what?"

She took a sip. She looked at the yard. Then she said the three words we would hear all day: "Work it out."

On the fridge, under the pineapple magnet, was a sticky note in her handwriting. *Iceland. 24 October 1975. Look it up.*

**7:40 a.m.**

Dad made the lunches. Mia's was an apple and a cheese slice, still in its plastic, rattling around a sandwich bag. Mine was two pieces of bread with nothing between them because, as Dad put it, "the important thing is that it's there."

Then Mia remembered the zoo excursion. The permission slip had to be signed, and paid for, and only Mum knew where it was.

"Where's the form?" Dad called through the flyscreen.

"Work it out."

Mia cried. Dad swore. And I said what we were all thinking, loud enough for the back step to hear: "This is so selfish."

Mum didn't turn around. Later, I would understand that this was the hardest part of her protest. Not the sitting still. The not getting up.

**11:15 a.m.**

In Maths, I looked it up.

On Friday 24 October 1975, about ninety per cent of the women in Iceland stopped. Not just their jobs. Everything. No cooking, no cleaning, no minding the kids. Twenty-five thousand of them, a tenth of the whole country, filled a single square in Reykjavík. Schools closed. Factories stood still. The phones went dead.

The organisers called it a "day off", because a woman could be sacked for striking. Even their protest had to be disguised as a holiday.

The shops sold out of sausages, the one meal the men could be trusted to heat up. The men had their own name for it: the Long Friday. I suppose it depends which side of the kitchen you were standing on.

Within a year, Iceland had passed an equal rights law. Within five, it had become the first country to vote a woman in as president.

Fifty years ago today. Another Friday.

I sat there with my empty sandwich and did some different maths. I was sixteen. As far as I could work out, this was the first day off Mum had taken since I was born. We'd always said she didn't work Fridays. We just hadn't been counting.

**3:40 p.m.**

When I got home, Mia was unpacking the dishwasher.

"Somebody has to," she said.

She was ten. She already knew which drawer the batteries lived in, which night the bins went out, that the dog's worming tablet had to be hidden in cheese. Nobody had taught her. She'd just been watching. I'd been watching too, I suppose, just not that. Nobody had ever expected me to.

Then, for the first time all day, Mum spoke without being spoken to.

"Mia. Leave it. Come and sit with me."

So Mia did. She took a cushion from the couch and sat beside Mum, and the two of them watched the yard as if something important were happening in it. In Iceland, they'd had a square. Mum had a back step. Now she had a crowd of two.

That was when I realised she wasn't only protesting for herself. She was protesting for the ten-year-old who already knew where the batteries lived.

**6:30 p.m.**

Dad came home with a packet of snags from Woolies.

I laughed so hard I had to sit down. Fifty years and half a planet away from Reykjavík, my father was proving the women of Iceland right.

We ate them on white bread, with sauce, in silence. Mum's chair stayed empty. It's strange how much room an absence takes up at a table.

"Longest Friday of my life," Dad muttered, stacking the plates.

"That's what they called it," I said. "The men in Iceland."

He looked at me, then out the window at the back step, and for once he had nothing to say. Then he filled the sink.

**9:47 p.m.**

I took her a cup of tea. I think it was the first one I'd ever made her.

"Why didn't you just tell us?" I asked.

"Tell you what?"

I opened my mouth and found I couldn't finish. What would she have told us? That she was tired? Everybody's mum is tired. That was the trick of it: the work was so constant that it had stopped looking like work at all. It just looked like Mum.

"Explaining it," she said eventually, "would have been one more thing I did for you."

I asked if she was angry with us. She thought about that for a long time.

"You don't go on strike against people you've given up on," she said. "You go on strike against people you think can do better."

I had always imagined protest as something loud: crowds, placards, people shouting at Parliament House. Never a woman sitting very still on a back step with a cup of coffee. But some protests don't add anything to the world. They take something away, and make you see what was there all along.

I had called it selfish. Every protest looks selfish to the people it inconveniences, and inconvenience is the point. A protest that costs nobody anything isn't a protest. It's a suggestion box. And I had always assumed injustice needed a villain. It turns out all it needs is a sixteen-year-old who has never once wondered where the clean socks come from.

That night I wrote a list of everything that hadn't happened that day. It ran to two pages. I stuck it on the fridge, beside her note.

**Saturday, 6:52 a.m.**

The kettle. The toaster's spring. The radio, telling nobody about the Monash. This time, I heard all of it.

Mum was back in the kitchen. At the bottom of my list, in her handwriting, were three things I'd missed.

I thought that was the end of it. Then I read that on the very same Friday, fifty years after the first, the women of Iceland had walked out again. They'd won their law. They'd elected their president. And they were still walking out. One Friday, it turns out, is never enough.

The list is still on the fridge. Every few weeks, another line appears. These days, some of them are in my handwriting.

I'm still working it out.
