# Objects of Dissent: exam notes

**Form:** a museum exhibition, written as a welcome panel and five case labels.
**Purpose:** to inform (the labels), to reflect (the curator's notes), and to persuade (the ending).
**Voice:** a polished museum voice that slowly cracks. The curator's notes get longer and more personal until Case 5 is told entirely in the first person.
**Length:** about 970 words.

## The central idea

We condemn protest while it is inconvenient, and celebrate it once it is safe. Each case shows the gap between how a protest was treated at the time and how it is honoured now. The curator then realises they did the same thing to their own mother.

## Where to work in the title and stimulus

1. **Name the exhibition after the title.** For example, "*What Remains*: an exhibition in five cases." The title is then in your first line.
2. **Write a case for the stimulus.** If the stimulus is an object, a photo or an artwork, use a spare label below or write a new one with the formula. Put it in place of Case 3, or just before Case 5.
3. **If the stimulus is a quote,** put it on the welcome panel as the exhibition's epigraph, or have the curator quote it in a note.
4. **Use the curator's notes.** Any of them can name the title outright.
5. **Last line.** "This case is unlocked" can be rewritten around the title if that lands better.

## Formula for writing a new case on the day

1. **Object line:** *Object. Material. Place, year.*
2. **What happened:** two or three sentences on who, what and why.
3. **How it was treated then:** one sentence on what it cost them, or how people reacted at the time.
4. **How it's remembered now:** one sentence on the gap between then and now.
5. **Curator's note:** one sharp line about protest.

## Spare cases to swap in

**Umbrella. Hong Kong, 2014.** *Use for umbrellas, rain, police lines, a crowd versus the state.*
In September 2014, police fired tear gas at crowds demanding the right to elect their own leader. The protesters opened the umbrellas they carried for the rain and held them up as shields. For 79 days they occupied the city's streets. Several of the movement's leaders were later jailed.
*Curator's note: We like our protests with happy endings. This one doesn't have one yet. That is not a reason to leave it out.*

**Candle. Leipzig, 1989.** *Use for candles, light and darkness, vigils, walls, non-violence.*
On 9 October 1989, about 70,000 people marched through Leipzig, East Germany, after prayers at St Nicholas Church. Many carried candles. A candle takes two hands, one to hold it and one to shield the flame, so neither hand is free for a stone. Security forces were waiting. They did not fire. A month later, the Berlin Wall fell. One official is said to have admitted afterwards: "We were prepared for everything. Except candles and prayers."

**Two shopping bags. Beijing, 1989.** *Use for a lone figure, tanks or power, anonymity, censorship, photographs.*
On 5 June 1989, the day after troops cleared Tiananmen Square, a man in a white shirt stepped in front of a column of tanks. He was carrying two shopping bags, as if he had just been on his way home. Nobody knows his name, or what happened to him. In China, his photograph is still censored.
*Curator's note: These are not his bags. Nobody kept them. Protest does not always need a hero. Sometimes it only needs someone who will not move.*

**Photograph. Moree, New South Wales, 1965.** *Use for water, pools, children, buses and journeys, segregation. Put it just before Case 5.*
In February 1965, Charles Perkins and a busload of university students arrived at the Moree Baths, where a council by-law kept Aboriginal people out of the pool. They brought Aboriginal children from the local mission to the gate. A hostile crowd gathered. After hours of standoff, the ban was lifted, at least for a while.
*Curator's note: Pools have been places of protest in this country before. Ask my mother.*

**Apron. Reykjavík, 1975.** *Use for domestic images, women, work, absence. It reuses your* Long Friday *facts.*
On 24 October 1975, about 90 per cent of Iceland's women stopped working: not in offices, not in factories, not at home. Twenty-five thousand of them filled a square in Reykjavík. The organisers called it a "day off", because a woman could be sacked for striking. The men called it the Long Friday.
*Curator's note: This apron did nothing all day. That was the point.*

## Adapting to likely titles

- **What Remains / Traces / Remnants:** "Protest leaves things behind: a brooch, a badge, a square of cardboard. What it rarely leaves behind is the anger."
- **Memory / Forgetting:** lean on Case 1, "We remember the brooch. We would rather forget the broken windows."
- **Legacy:** lean on Case 2 (the apology and statue arriving after death) and the Case 5 ending.
- **Hindsight / Time:** make the Case 3 note your key paragraph.
- **Unfinished / Ongoing:** lean on Case 4 and the last two lines.
- **Courage:** "Courage looks obvious behind glass. At the time, it looks like embarrassment."
- **Solidarity / Standing together:** lean on Case 2 and the Peter Norman callback in Case 5.
- **Small acts / Local:** lean on Case 5.
- **Voice / Silence:** "Not one of these objects can speak. Each of them was somebody's voice."
- **Symbols:** use the welcome panel line, "None of these things was made to change the world."

**Strong stimulus fit:** any object, photograph, poster, artwork or historical image, and quotes about memory, history, symbols, courage or hindsight.
**Weak fit:** domestic interiors (use *Long Friday*), and abstract or nature imagery (use #3, *Things That Refuse*).

## Facts used (checked October 2026)

- **"Suffragette":** first appeared in the *Daily Mail* in January 1906 and is generally read as belittling. The WSPU took it on, and in 1912 named its own newspaper *The Suffragette*.
- **The Holloway brooch:** designed by Sylvia Pankhurst. It shows the House of Commons portcullis and the prison broad arrow in purple, white and green. It was first presented on 29 April 1909 to women released from Holloway, and the practice stopped in 1914.
- **The vote in Britain:** some women got it in 1918, and women got it on equal terms with men in 1928.
- **Peter Norman, 16 October 1968:** wore the Olympic Project for Human Rights badge on the podium. He suggested Smith and Carlos share gloves, and "I'll stand with you" comes from Carlos's account. He was not picked for the 1972 Olympics; the Australian Olympic Committee disputes that this was punishment, which is why the text says only that he "never ran at another Olympics". He died in 2006, with Smith and Carlos among his pallbearers. The House of Representatives passed an apology motion in October 2012, and his statue outside Lakeside Stadium in Albert Park was unveiled on 9 October 2019.
- **Climate strikes:** Scott Morrison said "more learning in schools and less activism in schools" in Parliament in November 2018. About 300,000 Australians joined the 20 September 2019 strike, though that figure is an organiser and media estimate.
- **Aboriginal Tent Embassy:** set up on 26 January 1972 by Michael Anderson, Billy Craigie, Tony Coorey and Bertie Williams, from Redfern, with a beach umbrella and an "Aboriginal Embassy" sign on the lawn opposite Parliament House. Police removed it in July 1972, it went back up, and it was removed again. It has been there permanently since 1992. In 1995 it was placed on the Register of the National Estate, the only site on the register noted for its political significance to Indigenous Australians. Keep this label factual and don't invent quotes from the founders.
- **Spare-case facts:** the Hong Kong occupation lasted 79 days in 2014, and Benny Tai and Chan Kin-man were jailed for 16 months in 2019. In Leipzig on 9 October 1989, about 70,000 marched and no force was used. The "candles and prayers" line is usually attributed to Horst Sindermann but its exact wording is unverified, so keep "is said to have". Tank Man's identity is still unknown. At Moree in 1965, a council by-law excluded Aboriginal people from the baths, and the ban was lifted temporarily.
- **Fictional:** the curator, the mother, the pool, and the Case 3 placard (its slogan is a common climate-strike sign).
- **Dates to update:** "now eighteen" in Case 3 and "twenty years" in Case 5 assume 2026. Update them if you use the piece in a later year.
