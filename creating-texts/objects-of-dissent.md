# Objects of Dissent

*An exhibition in five cases*

**Welcome**

Every object in this room was once a nuisance.

A brooch worn by women the newspapers mocked. A badge that may have cost an Australian sprinter his career. A square of cardboard carried by a child who was told to go back to class. None of these things was made to change the world. Each was carried by someone who refused to accept the world as it was.

*Objects of Dissent* celebrates the ordinary things people have carried into extraordinary moments, and the long and proud history of protest they represent.

Please do not touch the glass.

**Case 1**
**Brooch. Silver and enamel. London, 1909.**

In 1906, a London newspaper coined a word to belittle the women demanding the vote: *suffragettes*. The women took the insult and made it their name.

They chained themselves to railings. They smashed windows. Hundreds went to prison, and when they refused to eat, they were held down and force-fed through tubes. When they walked out of Holloway Prison, the movement presented them with this brooch.

It shows the portcullis of the House of Commons, crossed by the arrow stamped on prison uniforms. Locked out of one; locked inside the other. What the government called a criminal record, the movement turned into a medal.

Some British women won the vote in 1918, the rest in 1928. Today the suffragettes appear on statues and stamps. We remember the brooch. We would rather forget the broken windows.

**Case 2**
**Button badge. Mexico City, 1968.**

On 16 October 1968, two African-American sprinters, Tommie Smith and John Carlos, stood on the Olympic podium and raised black-gloved fists against racism in the United States. Beside them stood the silver medallist, Peter Norman, a white sprinter from Melbourne, wearing this badge: *Olympic Project for Human Rights*.

It was not his fight. He made it his. When Carlos realised he had left his gloves behind, it was Norman who suggested the two Americans share Smith's pair. "I'll stand with you," he told them.

Smith and Carlos were sent home. Norman never ran at another Olympics. In 2012, the House of Representatives apologised for how Australia had treated him. In 2019, Melbourne gave him a statue. Both came too late. He had died in 2006, and Smith and Carlos had carried his coffin.

*Curator's note: We are very good at honouring protesters. We are best at it once they are dead.*

**Case 3**
**Placard. Cardboard and texta. Melbourne, 2019.**

*I'LL DO MY HOMEWORK WHEN YOU DO YOURS*

On 20 September 2019, around 300,000 Australians walked out of schools and workplaces to demand action on climate change. Many of them were children. The year before, as students prepared for their first walkout, the Prime Minister told Parliament what he wanted instead: "more learning in schools and less activism in schools".

Made by an eleven-year-old. Lent by its maker, now eighteen.

*Curator's note: I can already tell you how this label will read in fifty years. It will call these children brave. It will call them right. And the people who told them to stay in class will insist they were on their side all along. It is the pattern of every case in this room. We condemn protest while it is inconvenient, and celebrate it once it is safe.*

**Case 4**
**Empty.**

This case was built for a beach umbrella.

On 26 January 1972, four young Aboriginal men from Redfern pushed a beach umbrella into the lawn opposite Parliament House in Canberra and put up a sign: ABORIGINAL EMBASSY. If they were going to be treated as foreigners in their own country, they reasoned, they would need an embassy.

That July, police tore it down. Supporters put it back up. Police tore it down again. Since 1992, the Aboriginal Tent Embassy has stood on that lawn without a break. In 1995, it was placed on the Register of the National Estate. Honouring a protest, it turns out, is not the same as answering it.

*Curator's note: We built this case for that umbrella. Then I understood why it had to stay empty. A museum is where protest goes once we have decided how its story ends. This story has not ended. It does not belong behind our glass. It belongs on the lawn.*

**Case 5**
**Placard. Corflute and texta. Outside the town hall, every Thursday, 1997.**

*SAVE OUR POOL*

On loan from the curator's mother.

I was eleven when my mother made this. The council had voted to close our local pool, and every Thursday for a year she stood outside the town hall holding this sign. Some weeks, twenty people stood with her. Most weeks, it was three.

She brought me along after school. I waited in the car, parked around the corner, so nobody from school would see. Once, when a boy from my class walked past, I hid in the footwell. Peter Norman stood beside two strangers in front of the whole world. I couldn't stand beside my own mother in front of Year 6.

She lost. The pool closed in 1998. It is a car park now.

No museum would ever ask to borrow this placard. It changed no laws and won no medals, and nobody will ever build it a statue. That is exactly why it is here. Most protest looks like this: small, unglamorous and unsuccessful.

I have spent twenty years putting protest behind glass. Glass makes things beautiful. It also makes them safe. Every object in this room was once a nuisance, an embarrassment, a crime. Behind glass, you can admire them without feeling any of that. If you want to see what protest really looks like, don't look in here. Look for whatever embarrasses you.

My mother lent me this placard on one condition: that I give it back by Thursday. The council is selling the car park, and she wants her pool back.

This case is unlocked.
