# Things That Refuse: exam notes

**Form:** seven short numbered scenes, then a closing reflection.
**Purpose:** to express and reflect. It also argues for a wider idea of what counts as protest.
**Voice:** a reflective teenager who starts out believing they've never protested.
**Length:** about 900 words. It's short on purpose: adding a scene for the stimulus on the day brings it to about 1,000.

## The central idea

Protest is "the word no, said for long enough to be heard." It isn't only marches and placards: refusing, enduring and staying put are protest too. And every no is also a yes to something.

## How it holds together, so it doesn't read as a list

- **The repeated line:** "Nobody calls this protest" (scenes 1–3), then "Everybody calls this protest now" (4–5), then "Nobody calls this protest" again (6), and finally "Nobody calls this protest. I have started to." (7). The quiet refusals and the famous ones are the same act; the only difference is who calls them protest.
- **The name thread:** the council man misspelling Grandma's name (3), the joke about the new kid's name (6), the speaker's own name (7), and "everyone who carried it here" (ending).
- **One refusal leads to the next:** the boy who didn't laugh (6) is why the speaker finally says no (7).
- **Circular ending:** it opens and closes on the dandelion: "Give it till spring."

## Where to work in the title and stimulus

1. **Add a scene for the stimulus.** This is your strongest option. Use the formula below or a spare scene. Put it before scene 3 if it's about nature, or before scene 6 if it's about history or the wider world.
2. **Opening line.** It can carry the title, e.g. "I used to think [title] meant crowds and placards..."
3. **The repeated line.** It can take in the title, e.g. "Nobody calls this [title]."
4. **The ending's list of no's and yeses.** Add the stimulus to it.
5. **Last line.** Rewrite it around the title if that lands better.

## Formula for writing a new scene on the day

1. Something that refuses: a thing, a person or a place.
2. What it's up against: concrete, a government, a rule, a crowd.
3. How it keeps refusing: persisting, staying silent, staying put.
4. One sharp line about protest.
5. The repeated line: "Nobody calls this protest" if it's quiet or everyday, or "Everybody calls this protest now" if it's famous.

## Spare scenes to swap in

**Stockholm, 2018.** *Use for a lone figure, young people, signs, school, climate.*
On 20 August 2018, a fifteen-year-old skipped school and sat down outside the Swedish parliament with a hand-painted sign: SKOLSTREJK FÖR KLIMATET. School strike for climate. She came back every school day until the election, then every Friday after that. Within a year, millions of people around the world were walking out with her.
Everybody calls this protest now. It started with one girl and a sign.

**A redwood, California, 1997.** *Use for trees, forests, height, endurance, nature versus industry.*
In December 1997, a young woman climbed a redwood about a thousand years old to stop it being logged. She did not come down for 738 days. She lived on a small platform high in its branches, through storms, through two winters. In the end, the timber company agreed to spare the tree.
Everybody calls this protest now. She just called it not coming down.

**Tallinn, 1988.** *Use for music, voices, choirs, crowds, joy.*
In 1988, Estonia was part of the Soviet Union, and some of its songs were banned. That summer, crowds began gathering at night at the Song Festival Grounds in Tallinn to sing them anyway. By September, around 300,000 people stood together singing, nearly a quarter of the country. You can arrest a speaker. It is much harder to arrest a choir. Three years later, Estonia was independent.
Everybody calls this protest now. At the time, it sounded like singing.

**Hiroshima, 1945.** *Use for trees, war, destruction, renewal, time.*
After the atomic bomb in August 1945, people said nothing would grow in Hiroshima for seventy-five years. Within months, there were green shoots. Some trees survived the blast itself. The Japanese call them *hibakujumoku*, A-bombed trees. One, a ginkgo, still stands in a temple garden barely a kilometre from where the bomb exploded.
Nobody calls this protest. I can't think of a better word.

**My grandfather.** *Use for light and dark, old age, illness, time, or a Dylan Thomas quote.*
When my grandfather was dying, the doctors said days. He took weeks. He refused the wheelchair, refused the hospital food, refused to let anyone else hold his tea. "Do not go gentle into that good night," a poet once wrote. "Rage, rage against the dying of the light." Grandpa never read it. He didn't need to.
Nobody calls this protest. Some protests are against things that can't be beaten. They are still worth making.

## Adapting to likely titles

- **Roots / Growth:** lean on scenes 1 and 3, e.g. "Some refusals grow from roots you can't see."
- **Cracks:** scene 1, e.g. "Every crack in the concrete is an argument the concrete is losing."
- **Still Here / Persist / Endure:** scenes 1, 2 and 4. "Still here" can replace the repeated line.
- **Silence / Quiet:** scene 6, e.g. "The loudest thing in the room was one boy not laughing."
- **Names / Identity / Belonging:** scenes 3 and 7.
- **Memory:** scenes 2 and 4.
- **Water / Tides:** scene 2, "Water is the softest thing there is."
- **Small acts / Ordinary:** scenes 6 and 7.
- **Power:** scene 6, "So does most injustice."
- **Voice:** scene 7, "I said my name, all of it."
- **No / Refusal / Defiance:** the whole piece.

**Strong stimulus fit:** nature (plants, cracks, trees, water, rain), abstract images, poems, and quotes about persistence, water, silence, identity or names. Also images of mothers or headscarves, buses, lone figures, classrooms.
**Weak fit:** domestic interiors (use *Long Friday*), museum objects and historical artefacts (use *Objects of Dissent*), war and state violence (use *Stepping Up* or *Resist and Persist*).

## Plot outline to memorise

- **Opening:** never been to a protest · placard, chant, crowd · thought I'd never protested · then noticed the things that refuse
- **1, dandelion:** car park crack · concrete poured so nothing grows · sprayed every spring, comes back · concrete has to win every year, dandelion only once · Nobody calls this protest.
- **2, creek:** in the 1800s Melbourne buried a creek · Elizabeth Street · drain, road, shops · hasn't forgotten · storm, ankle-deep then knee-deep, café chairs · softest thing, still winning · Nobody calls this protest either.
- **3, grandmother:** forty years · English for the bank, the chemist, the council man · counts in her first language · prices, stitches, stairs · gave up her street, sisters, name · kept her numbers · she'd laugh at you
- **4, the mothers:** 30 April 1977 · fourteen mothers · disappeared children · gatherings banned, told to move along · walked in pairs every Thursday · nappies as headscarves · an order to leave became a way of staying · *las locas* · organiser disappeared · 2,000+ Thursdays · Everybody calls this protest now.
- **5, Rosa Parks:** 1 December 1955 · Montgomery · stayed seated · arrested, fined $10 · the "tired" myth · secretary of her civil rights branch · "tired of giving in" · a tired woman is easier to forgive than a determined one
- **6, the party:** joke about the new kid · everybody laughed, I laughed · one boy didn't · only works if everyone joins in, so does injustice · joke hung there · next time, nobody laughed
- **7, my name:** the roll, a fence · something shorter · for years I said yes · this year no, because of the boy · said all of it · nearly right · a dandelion's worth · I have started to.
- **Ending:** every crowd begins with one person saying no · no, said long enough to be heard · every no is a yes (sun, children, language, everyone who carried it) · council sprayed again · Give it till spring.

## Make the personal scenes your own

The grandmother, the party and the name are written as the speaker's own life. Change the details to fit yours: the language, the relative, the setting. If the name scene doesn't fit you, swap in another everyday refusal of your own and keep its shape: "For years, I said yes... This year, I said no."

## Facts used (checked October 2026)

- **Elizabeth Street:** follows the line of Williams Creek, which was put into a drain under the street in the 1800s. The street still floods in heavy storms.
- **The first gathering:** on 30 April 1977, Azucena Villaflor and thirteen other mothers went to the Plaza de Mayo. Police ordered them to move, so they walked in pairs around the square.
- **The headscarves:** the mothers first wore their children's nappies as headscarves at a 1977 pilgrimage. Later the scarves were embroidered with their children's names.
- **"Las locas":** the military used the label to discredit them, and the mothers took it up as a badge.
- **Villaflor:** disappeared on 10 December 1977.
- **The Thursday walks:** passed number 2,000 in 2016 and have continued since.
- **Rosa Parks:** on 1 December 1955 she was arrested and fined $10, plus $4 in court costs. She had been secretary of the Montgomery NAACP since 1943. Her line was "No, the only tired I was, was tired of giving in" (*Rosa Parks: My Story*, 1992).
- **Stockholm spare:** Greta Thunberg began on 20 August 2018 outside the Riksdag. She struck every school day until the 9 September election, then every Friday.
- **Redwood spare:** Julia Butterfly Hill spent 738 days in the redwood "Luna", from December 1997 to December 1999. The tree was saved by an agreement with the timber company. Sources disagree on the tree's age, so keep "about a thousand years".
- **Tallinn spare:** about 300,000 people sang at the Song Festival Grounds on 11 September 1988. Sources say that was a quarter to a third of Estonians, so keep "nearly a quarter". Independence was restored in 1991.
- **Hiroshima spare:** the "nothing will grow for 75 years" rumour was repeated by Hiroshima's mayor in 2020, and new shoots appeared within months. The Hosen-ji ginkgo is about 1.13 km from the hypocentre.
- **Grandfather spare:** Dylan Thomas's "Do not go gentle into that good night" was published in 1951.
- **Fictional:** the dandelion, the grandmother, the party, the speaker and the grandfather.
