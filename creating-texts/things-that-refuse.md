# Things That Refuse

I have never been to a protest. I have never held a placard, or chanted, or stood in a crowd that knows all the words. For a long time, I thought that meant I had never protested at all.

Then I started noticing the things that refuse.

**1.**

There is a dandelion in the car park behind the supermarket, growing out of a crack in the concrete. The concrete was poured so that nothing would grow there. Every spring, a council worker sprays the crack. Every spring, the dandelion comes back: first a fist of leaves, then a yellow head, then a hundred seeds on the wind.

It does not argue with the concrete. It doesn't need to. The concrete has to win every year. The dandelion only has to win once.

Nobody calls this protest.

**2.**

In the 1800s, Melbourne buried a creek. It ran down the gully where Elizabeth Street is now. The city sealed it into a drain, raised the road over the top and built shops along both sides.

The creek has not forgotten. Every so often, a summer storm breaks over the city and the water comes back the way it always came: down Elizabeth Street, ankle-deep, then knee-deep, carrying café chairs towards the river, taking the path it took for thousands of years before there was a city in the way.

Water is the softest thing there is. It is still winning.

Nobody calls this protest either.

**3.**

My grandmother has lived in this country for forty years. She learned English for the bank, the chemist, and the man at the council who never once spelled her name right. But she still counts in her first language. Prices, stitches, the stairs up to our flat: all of it in the words she was born with.

She gave up a great deal to come here. Her street. Her sisters. The sound of her own name said properly. Her numbers, she kept.

Nobody calls this protest. She would laugh at you if you did.

**4.**

On 30 April 1977, during Argentina's military dictatorship, fourteen mothers walked into the Plaza de Mayo in Buenos Aires to ask where their children were. Their sons and daughters had been "disappeared": taken, and never seen again. Gatherings were banned, so police ordered the women to move along.

So they moved. They walked in pairs around the square, and came back the next Thursday, and the next, in white headscarves. The first scarves were their children's nappies. An order to move along had become a way of staying. The generals called them *las locas*, the madwomen. The mothers took the name and kept walking. The woman who led the first march was herself disappeared before the year was out.

More than two thousand Thursdays later, they are still walking.

Everybody calls this protest now.

**5.**

On 1 December 1955, in Montgomery, Alabama, a bus driver ordered a Black seamstress to give up her seat for a white passenger. She stayed where she was. She was arrested and fined ten dollars.

The story we like to tell is that Rosa Parks was simply tired: a quiet woman with sore feet who had had enough. She spent years correcting it. She was secretary of her local civil rights branch, and she knew exactly what she was doing. "The only tired I was," she wrote, "was tired of giving in."

Everybody calls this protest now. But we still prefer the version where she was tired. A tired woman is easier to forgive than a determined one.

**6.**

At a party last year, someone told a joke about the new kid: his accent, his lunch, the way his name sounded. Everybody laughed. I laughed. One boy didn't. He looked down at his drink and waited.

A joke like that only works if everyone joins in. So does most injustice. With one person not laughing, the room noticed itself. The laughter thinned, then stopped, and the joke just hung there with nowhere to land.

Nobody calls this protest. But the next time that joke came round, nobody laughed.

**7.**

Every year, on the first day back, a new teacher reaches my name on the roll and stops, as if it were a fence. Then they look up and offer me something shorter. "Can I just call you...?"

For years, I said yes. It made the pause go away.

This year, I thought about the boy at the party, and I said no. I said my name, all of it, the way my grandmother says it, and waited while my teacher tried it twice, then three times, and got it nearly right.

It is a small thing. A dandelion's worth.

Nobody calls this protest. I have started to.

\* \* \*

I used to think protest needed a crowd. Now I think every crowd begins with one person saying no, and meaning it for long enough. That is all protest is, in the end: the word no, said for long enough to be heard.

But every no on this list is also a yes. The dandelion's no to the concrete is a yes to the sun. The mothers' no to forgetting is a yes to their children. My grandmother's no is a yes to the language she dreams in. And when I say my name, all of it, I am saying yes to everyone who carried it here.

Behind the supermarket, the council has sprayed the crack again.

Give it till spring.
